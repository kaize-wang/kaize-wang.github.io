Sometimes a paper has data, theory, and references, but still feels thin. I have been trying to understand where this feeling comes from.

## The confusing comment: “not deep enough”

“The content is there, but the paper lacks depth.” This is a comment that can be frustrating, because it sounds both clear and unclear. It is clear because the reader is not satisfied. It is unclear because the paper may already contain many things: background, literature, data, models, and conclusions. If all of these are present, what exactly is missing?

At first I tended to connect depth with difficulty. A deep paper should use more advanced theories, more complicated methods, or a larger narrative. But this explanation is not satisfying. A paper can be technically complicated and still feel shallow. Another paper can use simple language and modest methods, yet feel insightful. So the problem is probably not only about complexity.

## Description is only the first step

A lot of writing stops at description. It tells the reader what happened: a policy changed, a technology was adopted, a platform became more efficient, a group behaved differently. These descriptions are useful because they give the reader a place to stand. But if the paper stops there, the reader may still feel that the analysis has not really begun.

The next step is to ask why. Why did this change appear? Why did it appear in this way rather than another way? Why did it work in one setting but not in another? Once these questions appear, the paper begins to move from description to explanation.

## Mechanism is a way to connect the middle

I used to think of “mechanism” as a formal academic word. Now I understand it in a more practical way: mechanism is the missing middle between a cause and a result. It answers the question of how one thing becomes another.

For example, saying that a digital tool improves governance efficiency is only a beginning. A mechanism-oriented analysis would ask: Did it reduce information delay? Did it change the division of labor? Did it make some actors more visible and others less visible? Did it shift decision power from one place to another? These questions make the phenomenon more concrete.

This is also where the paper’s depth begins to appear. The reader can see not only that something changed, but also how the change was produced. The paper no longer simply points at a result; it reconstructs the path toward that result.

## A possible workflow for deeper writing

If I turn this into a writing process, it may look like this. First, identify the phenomenon and describe it clearly. Second, turn the phenomenon into a puzzle: why is it surprising, inconsistent, or worth explaining? Third, propose a possible mechanism. Fourth, ask what conditions make the mechanism stronger or weaker. Fifth, look for evidence that can support or challenge the mechanism.

This process is slower than simply writing down an answer, but it is probably more reliable. It prevents me from jumping too quickly from phenomenon to conclusion. It also reminds me that a good paper is not only a container of information, but a path of reasoning that the reader can walk through.

## A reminder to myself

When I feel that a paragraph is too flat, I should not immediately add more concepts. I should first ask whether I have explained the middle process clearly. What is the phenomenon? What is the puzzle? What is the mechanism? What evidence makes the mechanism believable? What boundary conditions should be admitted?

Maybe depth is not a mysterious quality. It is the result of patiently staying with the question for a little longer than the first answer.
