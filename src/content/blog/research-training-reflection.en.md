This is a more personal note about the gap between what I want to do and what I am currently able to do, and about how to rebuild a concrete research process.

## The uncomfortable mismatch

Recently I have often felt a mismatch: the problems I want to work on are larger than my current preparation. This is not simply a matter of working harder. It is more like standing in front of a task and realizing that I do not yet have enough concepts, examples, techniques, or judgment to move through it smoothly.

This feeling becomes especially clear when I revise a paper. On the surface, revision looks like changing sentences, adding references, or fixing proofs. But once I really enter the details, I find that every small change may require a chain of judgments: whether an assumption is reasonable, whether a theorem can be used, whether an error term is controlled correctly, and whether a proof route matches the problem.

## The theoretical gap is not abstract

For example, when I think about statistical error and approximation error, I quickly meet topics such as nonparametric estimation and statistical learning theory. I have seen terms like covering numbers, VC dimension, and pseudo-dimension, but seeing them is not the same as understanding how they work. I do not yet have a stable sense of when each tool should be used, what assumptions it needs, or how it changes once the setting is modified.

This makes reading papers difficult in a very concrete way. I may find a lemma that looks relevant, but I cannot easily tell whether it can be moved into my own assumptions. I may see a proof technique, but I do not know which part is essential and which part is only convenient for that paper’s setting. In that moment, reading is no longer just reading. It becomes a test of judgment.

AI can help me understand unfamiliar terms and outline possible routes, but it cannot replace the final responsibility. If I cannot explain why a step is valid, then the step is not really mine. This is the boundary I need to keep reminding myself of.

## What I lack is also a process model

Besides theory, I also lack a concrete model of the whole research process. A finished paper is an outcome, but it hides the path that produced it. How was the problem positioned? How were references selected? How did the author decide which experiment was necessary? How did the proof develop from a rough idea into a formal argument? These are the parts I most want to see clearly.

If I only know the target but not the path, I may try to jump directly to a polished output. That creates pressure, and it also delays communication. I may keep working alone because the current version does not look good enough, but by the time it looks presentable, the direction may already have gone wrong. Research probably needs earlier, smaller, and more frequent feedback.

## A research workflow I want to practice

A more concrete workflow may begin with problem clarification. Before searching for more papers, I should first write down what exactly I do not understand: is it a modeling issue, an identification issue, a proof gap, or an experimental design problem? Naming the type of problem may already reduce some confusion.

Then comes literature mapping. Instead of collecting papers loosely, I should ask what role each paper plays. Some papers define the problem, some provide a proof technique, some offer an experimental template, and some serve as contrast. If I can assign roles to papers, the literature will no longer be a pile of PDFs.

After that, I need to choose a technical route and write down its assumptions, even before the proof is complete. What do I need to show? Which existing result might help? Which assumption is the weakest part? Where could the argument fail? This turns proof writing from a mysterious activity into a sequence of checkable steps.

Finally, communication should happen before everything is polished. A short note with the current question, attempted route, obstacle, and next plan may be more useful than a long document produced too late. This is also a habit I need to train.

## Learning under uncertainty

The hardest part may be accepting that research does not feel like coursework. Coursework has chapters, exercises, and visible endpoints. Research often begins with not knowing what should be learned. This uncertainty makes me anxious, but it may also be the place where research ability is formed.

For now, I want to make the uncertainty smaller by making the process more visible. Write down the question. Map the literature. State the assumptions. Record failed routes. Ask for feedback earlier. Use AI for explanation and checking, but keep the core judgment in my own hands.

Maybe research training is not one sudden breakthrough. It is the slow building of a system that helps me keep moving when the next step is not obvious.
